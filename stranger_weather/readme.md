## Abstract

Semantics underneath the definitions of weather anomalies can be exploited to answer questions such as: which regions are facing accelerated warming, is the weather deviating more from its seasonal or monthly expectations, and, importantly, *does it matter?* This paper, based on evidence from four hundred German counties between 1995 and 2019, finds that these distinctions are non-trivial.

Regional warming above respective upper thresholds has not been uniform across Germany; by updating these thresholds annually, this paper finds that southern counties are warming faster than the north. This accelerated warming, above seasonal moving thresholds, is associated with a 0.11 percentage point reduction in the growth rate of GDP per-capita. Alternatively, above monthly thresholds, it is associated with 0.13-0.24 percentage points reduction; here the upper bound of losses arises when the share of days with conservative temperatures are included in the average annual warming.

On annual fluctuations in temperature, the seasonal mean temperatures deviate roughly 0.03°C more than monthly mean temperatures around their respective annual means. Subsequently, the growth loss associated with a 1 standard deviation increase in within-year variability is 0.70 (seasonal) and 0.43 (monthly) percentage points, averaging over fixed and moving reference periods.

The question - "does it matter" - remains central to the interpretations presented in this paper. In the end, this paper attempts to contribute to existing climate discourse, the assessment that how weather or climate anomalies are defined, is critical for measuring climate risk, identifying geographical vulnerabilities and estimating potential economic losses, with the goal of informing diverse economic decisions.
